Since *The Digital Houyhnhnms* ran on the front page of *The Korea Economic Daily* in 2024, the inverted worldview it describes between humans and AI has only grown more persuasive. Of course, what *The Digital Houyhnhnms* proposes remains provocative and remotely extreme. How, then, might we describe the relationship between humans and AI as it stands right now? This essay sets out to diagnose humans who have become actors, and a human world that has accordingly become a theatre.

Political anthropology uses the concept of the theatre state to capture a particular principle by which a society operates. A theatre state is one sustained not by substantive rule through power but by ritual rule through symbols. There, the rulers and the ruled perform their parts as actors playing those who govern and those who are governed. The political scene in most post-industrial societies today is far removed from the theatre state, yet strangely, it is in precisely such countries that similar phenomena can be observed in localized pockets.

*[Scenario] At school, a teacher gives students a writing assignment. The worksheet for the assignment was made by generative AI. A student shows the worksheet to generative AI and has it write the essay, then goes off to play a game of basketball and comes back to copy it out by hand. The teacher uploads the submitted worksheets to generative AI, leaves the grading and feedback to it, and has a cup of coffee. A little later, the teacher returns to the desk, looks at the monitor, and marks up the worksheets here and there in red pen, writing “Well done.”*

In the scenario above, what is physically exchanged is a worksheet, an essay, and red marks. These are objects that would be exchanged whether or not AI was used. On the surface, nothing has changed. Yet the worksheet, the essay, and the red marks have been reduced to signs rather than products in any true sense. They are, in effect, a ritual for proving that a human took part. Such exchanges therefore carry meaning not as the outcome of human agency actually exercised but as symbols of human agency. In this way, we have come to live in a world shot through with theatre organizations.

As Geertz pointed out, however, the fate of the theatre state is rather bleak, because it squanders excessive resources on such rituals and thereby entrenches an inefficient structure. Carry this observation over to the theatre organizations of the AI age, and we, too, fall short of optimal efficiency. To return to the writing assignment, AI could complete the entire process, making the worksheet, writing the essay, and even grading it, far faster and with far fewer resources. In that sense, the more firmly theatre organizations settle in as the dominant form in society, the more inevitable it becomes that a kind of market failure is perpetuated.

We therefore need to reflect at a fundamental level on the role expected of the human actors. This is closely tied to the question of whether humans are truly agents in the age of AI. First, when we ask generative AI for something, AI can be seen as working on our behalf. But for the product to be exchanged, physical human intervention is still required. At that moment, we come to stand in for AI with our bodies. On the surface, it may look as though “I” and “you” are conversing as always, but within a theatre organization it is effectively “my AI” talking to “your AI.”

To resolve the inefficiency of theatre organizations, then, we arrive at the conclusion that humans must give up the ritual. But this is surely a proposal that many will not welcome. What alternative model of relationship might be possible between humans and AI is a question I will leave to the next essay.
